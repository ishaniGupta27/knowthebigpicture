Boeing agreed to plead guilty to a criminal fraud conspiracy charge tied to two crashes of its 737 MAX jets that together killed 346 people. The plea deal, disclosed in July 2024, brands the company a convicted felon and requires it to pay a fine and submit to an independent monitor.

The story began in 2011, when Boeing decided to update its aging 737 rather than design an all-new aircraft, racing to match a fuel-efficient jet from its rival Airbus. To make the larger engines fit, engineers moved them forward and up on the wing, which changed how the plane handled.

To compensate, Boeing added a piece of flight-control software called MCAS that could automatically push the plane's nose down. The company decided not to describe the system in detail to pilots or in most manuals, treating it as a minor addition rather than a significant change.

In October 2018, a Lion Air 737 MAX crashed into the Java Sea shortly after takeoff from Jakarta, killing all 189 people on board. Investigators found that a single faulty sensor had triggered MCAS, repeatedly forcing the nose down as the pilots struggled to regain control.

In March 2019, a second 737 MAX operated by Ethiopian Airlines crashed minutes after departing Addis Ababa, killing all 157 people aboard. The similarities to the Lion Air disaster prompted regulators around the world to ground the entire 737 MAX fleet.

In January 2021, Boeing reached a deferred prosecution agreement with the U.S. Justice Department, paying over 2.5 billion dollars and promising to overhaul its compliance practices to avoid criminal prosecution.

In January 2024, a door plug blew off a 737 MAX 9 in mid-air during an Alaska Airlines flight, reigniting scrutiny of Boeing's manufacturing quality. Prosecutors later said the incident showed Boeing had violated the terms of its earlier agreement.

By pleading guilty in 2024, Boeing closed one chapter of its legal exposure but left families of the victims demanding a public trial and far harsher penalties for the company and its executives.
